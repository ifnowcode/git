Cybersecurity

Security is a compromise between ease of access and protection. I don't even implement all these rules just what I can and what makes sense to my setup.

Simple Cyber Safety Tips for Everyday Life

  • Use strong passwords: Make your passwords long and hard to guess. Avoid using the same one everywhere.
  • Turn on extra sign‑in protection: Some websites ask you to enter a code from your phone. This keeps your accounts safer.
  • Keep your devices updated: When your phone or computer asks to update, let it. Updates fix security problems. Always keep your web browser up-to-date.
  • Be careful with emails: Don’t click links or open attachments unless you are sure who sent them. This is especially true for sensitive information like bank accounts.Best to avoid the link entirely and visit said site manually in the browser.
  • Use your home Wi‑Fi safely: Make sure your Wi‑Fi has a password and isn’t open to strangers.
  • Don’t share personal details online: Be cautious about posting your address, phone number, or birthday.
  • Check your bank and credit card activity: Look for charges you don’t recognize.
  • Back up your important files: Save copies of photos and documents so you don’t lose them.
  • Be careful with public Wi‑Fi: Avoid doing banking or shopping on public networks like coffee shops. If you must use public wifi then use a VPN service.
  • Lock your phone and computer: Use a PIN, password, or fingerprint so others can’t get in.
  • Think before scanning QR codes: Only scan codes from places you trust. Don't trust if it appears tampered with.
  • Be cautious with phone calls: If someone calls asking for money or personal info, hang up and call the company directly.
  • Don’t install apps you don’t need: Some apps collect too much information.
  • Turn off Bluetooth when not using it: This helps keep your devices safer.
  • Turn off mobile WiFi when not home: If you can, turn off your mobile phone WiFi when not at home to protect from wireless sniffing attacks.
  • Examine credit card readers: Physically inspect card readers for loose, bulky or anything unusual especially when visiting unknown retailers.
  • Use credit cards online: Credit cards have more legal protections and allow more time and leverage to dispute charges when unsure about retailers.
  • Talk about scams: Let trusted allies know if something seems suspicious. It’s always okay to ask for help.
  • Don't use unknown storage: Sometimes bad actors will leave USB thumb drives laying around in places where they can be found to infect anyone naive enough to use it.
  • Hover over links before clicking: Hovering over links will popup (lower left usually) text that will show you the link before you click. Don't click if suspicious or non-existent.
  • Consider RFID-blocking wallet or purse: Contactless chips can be read via sniffing attacks. This risk is currently low though.
  • Be aware of social engineering: Most intrusions and scams are based on social engineering. It is still currently by far the #1 way people are compromised by bad actors.

Common Scams Targeting Older Adults

  • Grandparent scam: Someone pretends to be a grandchild in trouble and asks for money.
  • Tech support scam: A caller claims your computer has a virus and asks for remote access or payment.
  • Medicare or insurance scam: Someone asks for your Medicare number or personal details.
  • Prize or lottery scam: You’re told you won money but must pay a fee to receive it.
  • Utility or service scam: A caller threatens to shut off power or water unless you pay immediately.
  • Romance scam: Someone online pretends to form a relationship and then asks for money.
  • Charity scam: Fake charities ask for donations, especially after disasters.
  • Home repair scam: Someone offers cheap repairs, takes payment, and never finishes the work.
  • Bank or government scam: A caller pretends to be from your bank, the IRS, or Social Security.
  • Fake invoice scam: You receive a bill for something you never ordered.
  • Online shopping scam: Fake websites sell items that never arrive.
  • Investment scam: Promises of “guaranteed” returns or pressure to invest quickly.
  • Medical device scam: Offers for “free” medical equipment that require personal info.
  • Identity theft scam: Someone tries to collect your personal details to open accounts.
  • QR code scam: Scammers place fake QR codes that lead to harmful websites.

Top Cybersecurity Precautions Everyone Should Take

  • Use strong, unique passwords: Never reuse passwords across accounts.
  • Enable multi‑factor authentication: Prefer app‑based or hardware keys over SMS.
  • Keep devices updated: Install OS, browser, and app updates promptly.
  • Use a password manager: Avoid storing passwords in notes or browsers.
  • Secure your home Wi‑Fi: Use WPA3 if possible, strong passphrases, and disable WPS. Absolutely do not use anything less then WPA2.
  • Change default router passwords: Default credentials are widely known.
  • Disable unnecessary remote access: Turn off remote admin unless required.
  • Back up important data: Use both cloud and offline backups.
  • Use reputable antivirus/EDR: Keep it updated and active.
  • Beware of phishing emails: Verify links, attachments, and sender identity.
  • Avoid public Wi‑Fi for sensitive tasks: Use a hotspot or VPN instead.
  • Use a VPN on untrusted networks: Encrypts traffic and hides metadata.
  • Review app permissions: Remove apps that overreach.
  • Lock your devices: Use biometrics or strong PINs.
  • Enable device encryption: Protects data if the device is lost.
  • Monitor financial accounts: Set up alerts for unusual activity.
  • Use secure messaging apps: Prefer end‑to‑end encrypted platforms.
  • Limit personal info shared online: Oversharing fuels identity theft.
  • Check for data breaches: Rotate passwords if exposed.
  • Disable unused Bluetooth: Reduces attack surface.
  • Avoid installing random browser extensions: Many leak data or inject ads.
  • Use privacy‑focused browsers: Reduce tracking and fingerprinting.
  • Turn off ad tracking: Limits profiling and targeted scams.
  • Secure smart home devices: Change defaults, isolate on guest network.
  • Review account security settings: Many platforms hide critical options.
  • Be cautious with QR codes: They can redirect to malicious sites.
  • Use hardware security keys: The strongest form of MFA.
  • Freeze your credit: Stops identity thieves from opening accounts.
  • Educate family members: Your security is only as strong as the weakest link.
  • Regularly audit your digital footprint: Delete old accounts and unused services.
  • Do not use email accounts accessible by mobile for recovery: Create an email account for recovery only that isn't accessible via your mobile phone. This prevents attackers from accessing all your accounts if your phone is compromised.

"Trust nothing, verify everything"

Verify links: If there is typo in the domain name etc. don't click. E.g. yahhoo.com is not yahoo.com. You can trust Yahoo owns yahoo.com, but it has nothing to do with yahhoo.com. As such yahoo.com/... is owned by Yahoo. Be especially careful will links to you sensitive accounts like banks. Triple check if you are entering account details. Hover over links before clicking to see the URL. Never use links in email or links provided for sensitive accounts to protect against phishing. Go around and use your own links or type them in the URL bar yourself (do not bend on this rule!). Call the financial institution if you have to for example. Zero trust means you assume it's fraudulent or malicious until proven otherwise. Lastly never use http:// always verify it's https://. This again is especially true for sensitive online accounts.

Passwords: Do not use easy to guess passwords for online accounts. People who are still using 12345 or password etc. run great risk of compromise. Simple passwords that use one or two words then 69 for example are vulnerable to dictionary attacks. Strong passwords are not easy to remember or type in so a password manager is very helpful. Password managers typically can also generate strong passwords for you as humans are not random enough to reliably create strong passwords.

AI can now clone voices and there have been reports of scam calls where loved ones or work supervisors voices were cloned. Talk to your family or employers and decide on a pass phrase to let them know if they are legitimate or a fake over the phone. If you understand AI you can also try prompting the caller to see how they respond. You can also call them back on a trusted phone number if suspicious of the call I.e if they are making a request for money, sensitive data or are oddly urgent etc.
